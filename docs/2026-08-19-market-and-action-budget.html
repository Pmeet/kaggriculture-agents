Half our farm is walking

Three replays of ~3,200-rated agents, laid against our own game action for action. The gap is not strategy and not the crops we chose. We spend 56% of every action budget moving between tiles and only 27% doing work. They spend 42% and 41%.

The short version

We run their farm at half throughput — except where the market can't take it

Set our sell orders beside theirs and a pattern falls out immediately. On every product where the market can absorb volume we are at roughly half their number. On melon — the one product that collapses fastest under selling — we are at parity.

That single highlighted row is the whole thesis. We have optimised ourselves right up against a ceiling, and left the ceiling-free products at half.

Concept 01

Price impact: what it costs you to be the seller

Every unit you sell is added to a shared market inventory, and the price is a function of that inventory. So the second unit sells for slightly less than the first, and the thousandth may sell for nothing. Economists call this price impact, and a market where impact is small is called deep. It is the same reason selling one share of a company is easy and selling a million moves the price against you.

In this game the depth is not an emergent thing you have to discover by experiment — it is a single parameter per product, above_func, and it ranges over four shapes. That parameter, more than any crop statistic, decides what a product is worth to you.

Wheat and egg are log markets: sell four hundred units and the price is still 80% of where it started. Strawberry and milk are linear; wool and melon are square, and hit the $1 floor while you are still unloading your first harvest. There is no strategy that makes a square market absorb volume.

Concept 02 · marginal vs average

The headline price is a lie about the last unit

A crop's sticker price tells you what the first unit fetches. What matters for a decision to plant one more tile is what the next unit fetches — the marginal revenue. In a thin market those two numbers separate violently.

Melon's first 100 units earn $21,721 — about $217 each, the best line in the game. The next 300 units earn $5,006 in total, roughly $17 each. Same crop, same farm, one-thirteenth the value. Our agent holds 21 melon tiles and unloads them in a single late window, walking its own price from $277 on day 21 to $4 by day 27.
Concept 03 · stock and flow

The town is a conveyor, not a warehouse

The price depends on the inventory level (a stock), but the town removes goods at a fixed rate (a flow). Sell slower than the town consumes and the stock falls, so the price rises. Sell faster and it accumulates against you. The sustainable sales rate for a product is simply the rate the town drains it.

In the 137k replay, wheat inventory finished 436 units below equilibrium and the price rose from $25 to $46 — while the two best agents on the board were selling 431 and 433 wheat into it. They could not oversupply it if they tried. Wool in the same game sat 58 units above, and paid $5.
Concept 04 · the commons

You are selling into your opponent's market too

There is one inventory and one price, shared by both farms. Your opponent's dumping crashes your price as surely as your own. This makes production choices strategic rather than merely optimal: a product is worth what it is worth after the other farm has finished selling it.

This is why the melon parity in the opening table is worse than it looks. Two farms both maxing melon share one collapsing curve — and the fastest seller takes the high prices. Deep markets have no such race, which is exactly why they are where the reliable money is.
Concept 05 · opportunity cost

The scarce thing is a tile-day, and an action

Nothing here is profitable or unprofitable in isolation. A tile can hold one crop at a time, and a unit can take one action per turn, so the real question about any job is what you gave up to do it. Two budgets bind: about 75 usable tiles × 30 days, and about 7,000 unit-actions.

Concept 06 · duration

As the horizon shortens, only short crops are investable

A strawberry planted on day 6 has seventeen days of life and pays four times. The same seed planted on day 20 is a dead tile — the season ends before it yields. This is why the top agents' farms visibly change shape in the last third: land is rotated out of long crops and into the shortest one that still completes.

THUNDER THUNDER from day 22 to day 28: strawberry 29 → 7 tiles, while wheat climbs 31 → 44. They plant wheat 144 times in a game — a five-day crop cycled over and over. We plant it 102 times and finish with 40 ripe wheat units standing unharvested in the field.
Concept 07 · complements

Fertiliser is worth nothing without water

Inputs that only pay when used together are complements. The engine makes this literal: the fertiliser bonus is granted only on a day the tile was also watered. Applied properly it takes a wheat tile from 4 units to 6 — a 50% increase on the same land, the same seed and one extra action.

Our agent issues zero FERTILIZE actions. It collects 139 fertiliser from livestock and puts 138 of them up for sale into a market it has already glutted, where the price has fallen from $100 to about $46. The top agents collect ~310 and hold back ~70 for the fields.
The measurement

Where the action budget actually goes

All of the above would be advice we could not act on if we lacked the labour to act. So the last measurement is the plainest one: classify all ~7,000 unit-actions in a game as movement, idling, or work.

We take the same number of actions and convert 1,936 of them into work against THUNDER's 2,884 — 49% more work from an identical budget. Every downstream symptom follows from this one number: we harvest 203 times to their 389, feed 114 times to their 312, and fertilise zero times to their 70. There was never enough labour left over.

The two agents fail differently, which is useful, because it means the two levers are independent. THUNDER routes well (42.3% moving). HealthStone routes no better than us but almost never leaves a unit idle (9.4%). An agent that did both would reach roughly 48% working — about 3,400 productive actions, or 76% more than we manage now.

What I would do next

Ranked by expected value, not by how interesting it is

  1. Cut the walkinglargest

    Assign each unit a compact working area and keep it there for several turns, instead of sending whichever unit is cheapest to whichever job scores highest. The prize is ~950 extra productive actions a game, which is what every other item on this list needs in order to be possible.

  2. Turn fertiliser back oncheapest

    It is currently disabled outright. Applied to watered wheat it converts a $46 sale into two extra units worth ~$90, and we already collect the input for free. Smallest change in this list by a wide margin.

  3. Harvest before expiry, and rotate the dead tilemeasured

    We end games with ~40 ripe wheat units in the ground and a fifth of the farm idle. Strawberry dies on a known day — planted_day + 17 — so the replacement can be scheduled rather than noticed, exactly as the top agents do when they swing strawberry land into wheat after day 22.

  4. Re-price the crop mix on marginal, not average, revenuestructural

    Any planting decision that uses a product's current price will over-plant thin markets. It should use the price expected after our own projected supply lands — which is a change to how a tile is valued, not a new parameter to tune. The 15 August reprice moved this from a refinement to a prerequisite: it is what stands between us and the carrot/tomato premium below.

  5. Claim the carrot and tomato premiumlargest, and blocked

    At $91 and $241 a unit against wheat's $47, on land nobody else is using. Blocked on the item above and on the labour from item 1 — the one attempt made so far cost $34,044, so this needs the planner fixed first rather than a constant re-tuned.

Postscript · 15 August balance change

The rules changed under us, in exactly this area

Kaggle staff posted a stickied note titled Small balance change on 15 August; engine 1.32.7 went out an hour later. We were still running 1.32.6, so four days of measurement — including the first draft of this report — described a game the ladder had stopped playing. Confirmed rather than assumed: a replay of our own agent from 18 August disagrees with 1.32.6 on five sampled prices and with 1.32.7 on none.

The change adds a new curve shape, hinge, to the scarcity side of three products — calm until a product is genuinely short, then quadratic. Crops, animals, lifecycles and every glut-side curve are untouched, so Concepts 01–07 stand as written. What moves is the reward for supplying something nobody grows.

Nobody grows any of the three — not us, not the agents in these replays — so the deficits never close and the premium simply sits there. It also probably explains something the previous study got wrong: opponent banks jumped from ~$76k on 14 August to ~$100k on 18 August against frozen agent code, which was read as the field improving. 15 August is when the engine started paying several times more for three products.

What happened when we acted on it

Concept 02 collected its debt immediately

Our agent's internal price model was corrected to match the new engine, which is not optional — it decides what we sell. Letting those same prices drive planting measured −$34,044 a game over six seeds: the agent front-loaded carrot, the livestock build slipped behind it, and ten pastures finished the game empty.

The first fix — refusing to plan against the runaway part of the curve — changed nothing at all, because the deficits that appear in valuation sit below the knee. The damage came from carrot's scarcity target moving 0.20 → 1.00, which lifts the whole curve rather than the tail. A plausible fix, measured, worth exactly zero.

Selling now uses the true price; planting is pinned to the old curve until the planner can take the opportunity without dropping the livestock build. The premium is real and unclaimed — but it is an average price on a product we would be the only seller of, which is precisely the trap Concept 02 describes.

Caveats

What this study does not establish

  • The replays are from 11 August, before the balance change. These agents were at the top then; the mechanics they exploit are unchanged, but their crop mix predates a reprice that would reward a different one.
  • The depth table and chart are computed on the glut side of the curve, which 1.32.7 did not touch. They are current. The scarcity figures in the postscript are the ones that moved.
  • Their game and ours drew different shops, and shop draw swings a product's total demand by several hundred units. Comparisons of structure — action mix, walking, rotation — are sound. Direct comparisons of bank are not.
  • Sell figures are order requests, which over-count: a standing order repeats each turn but only fills while the shed has stock. Both sides are counted identically, so the ratios hold even though the absolute numbers are high.
  • The 49% and 76% headroom figures are arithmetic on the action census, not measured bank. They say how much labour is recoverable, not what it will be worth.
